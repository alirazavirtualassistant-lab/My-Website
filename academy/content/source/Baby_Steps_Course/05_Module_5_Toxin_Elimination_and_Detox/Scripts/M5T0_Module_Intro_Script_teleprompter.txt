— 0:30–2:00  WHY TOXINS MATTER, ACROSS GENERATIONS —

Let's start with the science that made this personal for me. In 2005, Michael Skinner's lab at Washington State University published a study in the journal Science showing that exposing pregnant rats to a common fungicide produced fertility problems not only in their sons, but in their grandsons and great-grandsons, through epigenetic marks on the sperm. That's animal research, and humans are not rats, but it opened a field. Environmental Health Perspectives, the journal of the U.S. National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences, has since published a stream of human studies linking everyday chemicals, plasticizers, pesticides, preservatives, to changes in sperm quality, egg quality, and hormone levels. In 2015, the Endocrine Society issued its second scientific statement on endocrine-disrupting chemicals, concluding that the evidence for their effects on reproduction was strong enough to warrant action. And the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, in its committee opinion on toxic environmental agents, encourages clinicians to talk with patients about reducing exposures before and during pregnancy.

Here's the hopeful part, and I'll keep saying it all module: exposure is not destiny, and many of these chemicals leave the body fast. Switch what you eat and what you touch, and the levels in your urine drop within days. The body clears. Our job is to stop refilling it.
