— 2:00–6:00  SCIENCE BASICS: THE 90-DAY WINDOW AND THE FIVE PILLARS —

Let's start with a number: ninety. Human sperm take about seventy-four days to be built, and then roughly two more weeks to travel and mature, according to the classic work of Heller and Clermont published in the journal Science and still cited by the National Institutes of Health today. Call it three months, start to finish. On the egg side, the follicle that will eventually release an egg spends its final months of growth in a similar window; the reproductive biologist Alain Gougeon mapped that stretch of development in the journal Human Reproduction. So here is the plain truth of it: the egg and the sperm that could become your child are being built right now, out of the raw materials of your ordinary days. The lunch you had. The sleep you got. The argument you didn't finish.

Now layer on epigenetics. Your genes are the alphabet; that part you can't change. Epigenetics is the punctuation, the chemical marks that tell a gene when to speak up and when to stay quiet. Those marks respond to nutrition, stress, sleep, and chemical exposures, and some of them are carried into the next generation. Researchers who studied families who lived through the Dutch Hunger Winter of 1944 found that people conceived during the famine still carried distinct epigenetic marks sixty years later, a finding published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. And the Överkalix study in northern Sweden, published in the European Journal of Human Genetics, linked how well-fed grandparents were as children to the health and lifespan of their grandchildren. Your choices reach further than you can see. I find that sobering and thrilling in equal measure, because it means the good you do now travels, too.

And here's the hopeful part. When Harvard researchers followed more than seventeen thousand women in the Nurses' Health Study II who were trying to conceive, they found that women who followed a particular pattern of eating and living, more whole foods, more healthy fats, more plant protein, regular movement, a healthy weight, had a sixty-six percent lower risk of ovulation-related infertility. That's Jorge Chavarro and colleagues, published in Obstetrics and Gynecology in 2007. Sixty-six percent. Not from a drug. Not from a procedure. From daily life, done with intention.

So in this course we organize daily life into five pillars. Nutrition: what you fuel with, and what you crave. Movement: how you circulate blood, oxygen, and mood. Stress: how your nervous system talks to your hormones, because it does, constantly. Toxins: what you're exposed to and what you can clear. And connection: the relationships that hold you steady, starting with your partner and reaching out to your community. Every module in this course strengthens one of these pillars, and they hold each other up the way the beams of a house do. Weaken one and the others carry extra weight. Strengthen one and every other pillar gets a little lighter.

— 6:00–8:00  SELF-REFLECTION: WHERE ARE YOU STARTING? —

Now I want you to think about where you are today, without judgment. In my work, the barriers I see most are not laziness or lack of willpower. I've never once met a lazy person who was trying to have a baby. What I see is stress that never switches off. Sleep that's too short or too broken to repair anything. Cravings that seem to come out of nowhere and win. Two partners who love each other but are on different pages. And an environment, the kitchen, the bathroom cabinet, the workplace, that quietly works against the person trying to change.

[PAUSE — let this land]

So here's a reflection prompt, and I'd love for you to write your answers if you have your journal. First: on a scale of one to ten, how much energy do you have most afternoons? Not mornings, afternoons, the honest hour. Second: which of the five pillars feels strongest for you right now, and which feels most neglected? Don't overthink it; your gut already knows. Third: what is one thing you've been waiting for that you could start preparing for instead?

Remember my client's word: prepare. The woman I told you about at the start didn't change everything. She changed her afternoon snack, because she noticed her energy crashed at three every day, and she moved her bedtime up by forty-five minutes. Two things. And for the first time in a year she told me she felt like a participant in her own story instead of an audience member. That was her aha moment. Yours will probably look different. But it starts with an honest look, and honesty is so much easier when there's no shame attached to it.
